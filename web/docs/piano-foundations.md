## Piano practice mindset to retain

1. **Automatic foundations free attention.** Keyboard landmarks, finger numbers, note recognition and rhythm need repeated use. Understanding their explanation is different from executing them comfortably while reading and coordinating hands. Do not turn a single successful attempt into permanent completion.
2. **Practise regularly with a manageable challenge.** The teacher repeatedly favors short, frequent practice, with foundations and exercises alongside current pieces. His minute/day/repetition examples are suggestions, not mandatory quotas, health guarantees or a universal learning schedule. Progress can be uneven without implying failure or low talent.
3. **Isolate, improve, reconnect.** Work on a difficult movement or bar, then practise its entry/exit and reintegrate it into the phrase. Repeatedly restarting an otherwise comfortable piece wastes attention on what is already secure. Balance focused work with playing music for enjoyment.
4. **Alternate separate and combined hands.** Hands-separate practice is not a phase to abandon once both hands are attempted. Supplementary A and the adult advice explicitly recommend alternating them and gradually changing the balance. Coordination, opposite articulation and dynamic balance are distinct tasks.
5. **Listen while playing.** Correct key order is only one dimension. Even touch, legato/staccato, phrase shape, melody prominence, dynamics and pedal choices make the result musical. A pitch detector must not imply that it has verified all of this.
6. **Count the rests as well as the notes.** A rest has an intended duration; it is not merely an arbitrary pause. Counting/subdivision can be learned separately from finding pitches. Early slow internal counting and later metronome practice serve different needs; neither is a universal requirement for every exploratory session.
7. **Plan movement and read relationships.** Previewing a score, noticing patterns, finger changes and upcoming jumps reduces surprises. This is appropriate in phrase reading. It must not introduce a next-target preview into the user's deliberately unpredictable reflex drill.
8. **Fingering serves comfort, efficiency and expression.** Thumb is 1 and little finger 5 on either hand. Exercise fingerings can be specified, but pieces often allow alternatives depending on hand, style and intended sound. Jazz crush notes and finger substitutions show why a universal one-note/one-finger rule is wrong. Physical fingering remains unverified by ordinary microphone or MIDI messages.
9. **Reading, memory, hearing and creativity reinforce one another.** Follow a score even after memorizing a piece, but do not call that fresh sight-reading. Singing/audiation, listening to varied music, improvisation and understanding harmony are complementary activities. The teacher's proposed cross-instrument benefits are hypotheses, not proof that another instrument or purchase is required.
10. **Work with the instrument and player available.** Key range, touch sensitivity, pedal availability, acoustic/digital behavior and comfortable reach vary. Early visual/keyboard learning need not require expensive equipment. Do not imitate one teacher's posture mechanically: he explicitly warns that his anatomy makes his own posture a poor universal example. Do not prescribe painful stretching or infer physical health from performance data.

## Caption conflicts and rules not to infer

- Lesson 14's `en` translation calls B-flat major/G minor **parallel** and sometimes changes B-flat to C-flat. The alternate says **relative**. Lesson 19 repeats this confusion, mixes Ukrainian text into English, and turns the raised B-flat in C harmonic minor into “B-flat major”; the intended change is B-natural. Track labels cannot settle musical truth.
- Automatic tracks corrupt clef, aural, semibreve, con pedale, note letters and chord names. Lesson 16's C triad becomes C–B–G in one caption span; its four example chords include E minor although another span repeats A minor. Never generate exercise targets directly from those sequences.
- The adult-strategy translation's “anchors,” “overperformance” and “counting” obscure reinforcement, continuing prior work and tallying repetitions. That tally is different from counting musical beats.
- Simplified explanations such as major=happy, minor=sad; “all music has beat”; compound meter=two ways of counting; or every F in a bar is affected without octave/tie context are teaching shortcuts. Do not encode them as universal rules. Key, accidental scope, tied continuation, voice and meter context need explicit notation definitions.
- Slur/tie, augmentation dot/staccato dot, beat/finger number, hand/clef, and notation/sounding duration are distinct concepts. Historical ornament names and editorial interpretations need review before rigid scoring.
- Teacher examples of practice durations, extra repetitions, adult/child learning rates, neuroplasticity and injury prevention are not evidence for personal forecasts, fixed quotas, health assessment, forced stretching or neurological/innate-talent labels.

## What this means for the current app

The owner confirmed using a digital piano/keyboard with USB/MIDI during this review. Treat that as user-supplied capability context, not a tested connection or proof of browser support. It makes optional MIDI feasibility a relevant next step; no MIDI functionality was implemented in this documentation task.

The inspected browser code still represents each target as one MIDI pitch with optional hand/finger and a level-wide hold. `p1`–`p6` provide middle-C, same-letter/octave, five-note and right-hand C-major practice; `p0` provides constrained random reflex. The `p6` target data is right hand only, despite broader “one hand at a time” goal wording. None of these establishes a complete piano syllabus.

`NoteValidator` latches after the first accepted short hold. `usePractice` then waits for about 0.18 seconds below the level gate before advancing. That deliberately isolated-note loop cannot assess connected legato, staccato duration, ties, pedal, simultaneous chords, multiple voices or a whole phrase. Piano decay and pedal resonance also prevent equating microphone silence with physical key release. The detector's current 100–2,000Hz range is not the full piano range.

The notation helper chooses clef by target range and prints sharp pitch-class spellings. It does not model an exercise's chosen clef independently, key signature, flat/enharmonic spelling, rests, voices, ties or phrase navigation. Those are future data-model capabilities, not evidence that current single-note Music sheet is a full score reader. Old session IDs and note evidence must remain readable when new modes arrive.

For future design, [MusicXML's notation tutorial](https://www.w3.org/2021/06/musicxml40/tutorial/notation-basics/) provides a primary example of separating sounding music from notation appearance. [The MIDI Association message summary](https://midi.org/summary-of-midi-1-0-messages) documents note-on/off and controller messages useful for future digital-keyboard evidence. Our inference is that these can support richer timing/polyphony evidence; neither source implies anatomical hand/finger detection or certifies musical interpretation.
